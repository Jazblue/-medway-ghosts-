📍 Downing Street — Prime Minister's Study — 22:47
The PM's study is smaller than the Cabinet Room, and at this hour it feels smaller still. Four people are present: the Prime Minister, the Deputy Prime Minister, the Defence Secretary, and the Chief of the Defence Staff, Admiral Sir Gwyn Jenkins. A single lamp on the desk illuminates a map of the Channel, the North Sea, and the approaches to the Strait of Dover. The curtains are drawn. Outside, August rain is hitting the windows in steady, heavy sheets. The Defence Secretary is still in uniform, her tie undone, the ribbons on her chest catching the lamplight. The Deputy PM is rubbing her eyes, the day's papers spread across the desk. The Chief of the Defence Staff is standing by the map, a glass of water untouched in his hand. The PM is pouring whisky. He does not offer a glass to anyone else.
PRIME MINISTER
Alright. Let's have it. What are we actually looking at?
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF (Admiral Sir Gwyn Jenkins)
Three days, Prime Minister. Three days they've been in our waters. Russian destroyer, two supply ships, a frigate hanging back as overwatch. They're not hiding. They're testing the edges of the exclusion zone we declared after the yacht incident. Every hour they stay is a message. The question is whether we answer it.
DEFENCE SECRETARY
The yacht incident. Jesus. We got lucky with the Kelveys. One gust of wind, one panicked helmsman, and we'd be explaining a British couple dead in the Channel on live television. The press would have eaten us alive. "Government risks pensioners to score points against Moscow" — that's the headline that writes itself. And the Russians know it. They were careful with that one. Calculated.
PRIME MINISTER
We know. Don't labour it. Gwyn — options.
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
Three. First: we shadow them out. Professional, legal, NATO-compliant. Takes them past the Strait of Dover, hands them off to the French in the Atlantic. Nothing happens. They win.
DEPUTY PRIME MINISTER
Wins what?
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
Wins the argument. They prove they can transit our waters whenever they like, with a Royal Navy escort that can't touch them. That's the signal to Kyiv, to Washington, to Moscow. That's the signal to every Baltic state watching our every move. If we do nothing, we're not preserving peace — we're purchasing delay at the cost of our credibility.
PRIME MINISTER
Option two.
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
Option two is the boarding. We have legal authority under UNCLOS Article 110. We board the supply ships — not the warship, that's a red line — and we inspect their cargo. If it's sanctions-busting material, we divert to Aberdeen. If it's not, we leave. But either way, we make them stop.
DEFENCE SECRETARY
With respect, Gwyn, that's a red line for them too. A boarding in international waters against a Russian-crewed vessel — they've already threatened "appropriate response" if we interfere with their flagged ships. Last month, their ambassador handed our Foreign Secretary a note that said, in so many words, any interference would be treated as an act of aggression. The wording was deliberately ambiguous. That's how they operate. They leave themselves room to escalate while maintaining plausible deniability.
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
They threatened. They haven't acted. And the ships are under sanctions. We've done it four times in the last year. The pattern is established. Each time, the Russians postured, sent a few jets into the Baltic, and then went quiet. They're not looking for a war. They're looking for a win in the narrative.
DEPUTY PRIME MINISTER
Four times is not many. And every time we've done it, the Americans have held their breath. One Russian casualty — even a heart attack on a bridge during a boarding — and we'd be in an emergency Security Council session before breakfast. Option three.
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
We don't have an option three.
A silence. The rain is louder now. Somewhere in Whitehall, a clock ticks. The PM sets his glass down with a sharp click that makes everyone look up.
PRIME MINISTER
Then we make one up. Because doing nothing is not an option. Not this time.
DEFENCE SECRETARY
With respect, Prime Minister, making things up is exactly how we end up in a war nobody planned. We need a legal pathway. We need NATO cover. We need the Americans publicly on board before we make a move that Moscow can spin as unprovoked aggression.
PRIME MINISTER
And while we wait for the legal pathway, the Russians sail past Dover with whatever they're carrying. Every day we delay is another day they get closer to arming the Northern Fleet. Every day we delay is another day the Ukrainians die with weapons we withheld because we were worried about a note from an ambassador.
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
I understand the frustration. But let me be precise about the military timeline. If we choose option two — the boarding — I can have a team on those ships within six hours. But I need a political decision now, because the ships are moving west. If they reach the Atlantic, our legal authority weakens. The farther they get from UK waters, the harder it is to justify interception.
DEPUTY PRIME MINISTER
Six hours. That's not a lot of time to build a coalition.
PRIME MINISTER
Then we don't wait for a coalition. We build it afterwards. We do the boarding. If the Russians respond, we deal with it. If they don't, we've proven we're serious. Either way, we've shifted the narrative.
The Defence Secretary shakes her head slowly. She leans back in her chair, the fatigue visible now that the adrenaline is fading.
DEFENCE SECRETARY
You're talking about a calculated risk. That's fine. I'm a politician too — I know what risks look like. But I need you to understand the full picture before we make that call. The Navy isn't just stretched; it's running on fumes. We have three destroyers operational. Three. And one of them is already tied up with the Russian group. If we go ahead with the boarding, and the Russians decide to escalate, we don't have depth. We don't have reserves.
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
That's correct. But I also have submarines in the area. I have maritime patrol aircraft. I have NATO assets that will move to our support the moment we invoke Article 5. We're not as naked as you think.
PRIME MINISTER
And if they don't invoke Article 5?
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
Then we handle it ourselves. We always do.
📍 22:53 — Same Room
PRIME MINISTER
We're not doing nothing again. I'm not taking that to the House. Last time we let them steam through the Channel, the papers were calling me weak for a week. The 1922 Committee is already muttering about resolve. The backbenchers are getting twitchy. And for what? So we can say we avoided escalation? The public doesn't care about escalation. They care that a Russian warship is sitting twenty miles off the Isle of Wight and we're sending a patrol boat to say "please don't." That's not leadership. That's negligence.
DEFENCE SECRETARY
With respect, Prime Minister, this isn't about the papers. It's about what happens when a destroyer meets a patrol boat with guns and no inhibitions. You want the honest version?
The PM nods.
DEFENCE SECRETARY
We don't have enough ships. We've been running the Navy at operational tempo since February. The Type 45s are tired. The frigates are doing three jobs at once. If we escalate — if we actually intercept — we risk a collision, a shot across a bow, something that neither side planned. And if that happens, we'd be relying on the Americans to hold the line with Russia. Are we sure they will?
PRIME MINISTER
I've spoken to the White House twice today. The President is supportive. But you're right — supportive isn't the same as committed. If a Russian sailor bleeds, and the Kremlin spins it as an act of war, we need to know what Washington does next.
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
I've asked for the same thing. What I'm hearing is conditional. Article 5 invoked? Yes. Something short of that? The Americans want Europe to take the lead. They're focused on the Pacific. They want us to carry the weight in our own backyard.
DEPUTY PRIME MINISTER
That's the real question, isn't it? Europe can't take the lead if we're the only ones in the water. France has carrier problems — their Rafales are grounded half the time, their only carrier is in refit. Germany's navy is coastguard-sized. We are the only country in this alliance with a surface fleet that can actually shadow a Russian task group for seventy-two hours without assistance. And even we're struggling.
The PM refills his glass. Everyone else drinks water or nothing at all. The Deputy PM leans forward, elbows on the desk, and pulls a document towards her.
DEPUTY PRIME MINISTER
Let me add something else. The Treasury is already calculating the cost of escalation. Not in blood — in pounds. If we board those ships and Russia responds with sanctions of their own, if they cut off our energy supplies further, if they launch a cyberattack on the City, the markets will react. The pound will move. Insurance premiums will spike. We could be looking at a billion-pound hit before the week is out. And that's before we start counting the cost to the supply chain.
PRIME MINISTER
And the cost of doing nothing?
DEPUTY PRIME MINISTER
The cost of doing nothing is that we lose the one thing we have left: credibility. If we let them sail past Dover with impunity, the Ukrainians will start making their own deals with Moscow. The Baltic states will start looking to Poland and Scandinavia for security guarantees. The alliance fractures not with a bang but with a series of small, pragmatic decisions by countries that no longer trust us to show up. That's the real cost. And it's not measurable in pounds.
DEFENCE SECRETARY
She's right. We've seen this before. The Suez crisis. The Falklands. Every time we hesitate, the world notices. The Russians are calculating our every move. They think we're weak. We need to prove them wrong.
PRIME MINISTER
So we're alone, we're stretched, and we're one miscalculation away from a war nobody wants. Great. Any other good news?
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
I have good news. The intelligence picture is clearer than it was this morning. We know the supply ships are carrying something. We just don't know exactly what. But the manifests are fake. The satellite imagery doesn't match the paperwork. That gives us grounds.
PRIME MINISTER
What kind of grounds?
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
Legal grounds. If the cargo doesn't match the manifest, that's a violation of the Maritime Labour Convention and the International Convention for the Prevention of Pollution from Ships. It's not a sanctions violation yet, but it's enough to board and inspect. Once we're on board, we find the real cargo, and then we have the sanctions violation we need.
DEFENCE SECRETARY
That's creative. That's also a pretext. And if the Russians claim we fabricated the evidence?
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
Then we have the satellite imagery, the intercepted communications, the whole picture. We don't need to fabricate anything. The Russians are sloppy. They always have been.
📍 23:05 — Intelligence
DEFENCE SECRETARY
Let me put something on the table that the Defence Committee hasn't heard yet. We have intelligence — not confirmed, but from a source we've used before — that the supply ships aren't carrying oil at all. Not this time. We believe they're carrying components. Missile guidance systems, perhaps. Dual-use electronics bound for the Northern Fleet.
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
I can't confirm that in this room.
DEFENCE SECRETARY
You don't have to confirm it. I'm telling the Prime Minister what I've been told. If that's accurate — if they're running weapons components in a sanctioned corridor — then this isn't just a sanctions breach. It's a weapons delivery. That changes the political calculus entirely.
PRIME MINISTER
And that changes the legal position?
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
It changes the political position. Legally, we're still in the same box. Operationally, it means we can't afford to look the other way. If those components reach the Northern Fleet, they could be used to target our submarines in the Arctic. This isn't abstract.
DEPUTY PRIME MINISTER
It also changes the message if we board and find nothing. Right now we can say we're enforcing sanctions — that's a legal argument with international backing. If we board and it's just grain or humanitarian aid, we look like the aggressor. The Russians would love that. They'd spin it as piracy. They'd take it to the International Court. They'd weaponise the law against us.
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
It's not grain. I can tell you that much.
DEFENCE SECRETARY
Can you?
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
Let's just say the cargo manifests don't match the satellite imagery. We've seen transfers at sea. We've seen the rendezvous patterns. This isn't a humanitarian convoy.
Another silence. Longer this time. The lamp flickers. Outside, a siren passes in the distance — Chatham, perhaps, or Gillingham.
DEPUTY PRIME MINISTER
If we board, and we find weapons components, what's the fallout? Russia won't just tut. They'll respond. They have assets in the Mediterranean. They have cyber capability. They have proxies everywhere. Last week, our intelligence services flagged a series of suspicious port activities in Tartus. Coincidence? I don't think so. They're moving pieces into place. If we touch those ships, we're touching a bigger game.
PRIME MINISTER
And if we don't board? If we let them carry those components to the Northern Fleet, and three months from now a British ship is hit by a missile with a guidance system we let through? How do we explain that to the families?
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
You can't. That's the burden of command.
DEFENCE SECRETARY
There's another angle here. The Americans. If we board and find weapons components, we hand it to them. They've been desperate for a reason to go harder on Russia's military supply chain. This gives them the pretext. They can sanction the manufacturers, freeze the assets, go after the intermediaries. We'd be doing their homework for them. And if they don't follow through? Then we've made our point anyway. We've enforced the law. We've shown we're not bluffing. That's worth something on its own.
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
And there's one more thing. We have a submarine in position. If the boarding goes badly — if the Russians try to ram us, if they fire warning shots — we have underwater assets that can intervene without being seen. That's my insurance policy. But I need you to know it's there, and I need you to accept that it escalates the risk. If a Russian vessel is damaged by something they can't see, the Kremlin will scream assassination. They'll say we attacked without warning. The diplomatic cost would be enormous.
PRIME MINISTER
Understood. We're not looking for a submarine war. But we're not letting them pass. Not with what they're carrying. Gwyn, I want you to draft the boarding order. I want it on my desk by 02:00. The Attorney General will review it. We'll have a final decision at 05:00. If we proceed, I want it done before dawn. No cameras, no leaks, no second chances.
DEPUTY PRIME MINISTER
And the message to Parliament?
PRIME MINISTER
We brief the 1922 Committee first. Then we make a statement to the House. We say the Royal Navy is conducting a sanctions enforcement operation in UK waters. We don't give details. We don't name the ships. We say the safety of British shipping is paramount. That's all they need to know until it's over.
DEFENCE SECRETARY
And if Parliament asks why we didn't inform them sooner?
PRIME MINISTER
Because operations in progress are not debated. They're executed. We'll have the full facts afterwards. Until then, the House trusts the Government to do its duty. If they don't, they can vote against us afterwards. That's how this works.
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
I'll prepare the order. But I need one thing in writing: the political authorisation to use force if the boarding party is threatened. I don't want my ships standing there with rules of engagement that protect the Russians but not my people.
PRIME MINISTER
You'll have it. Self-defence is non-negotiable. If they threaten your personnel, you respond. That's the instruction. And Gwyn — keep me updated every hour until this is resolved. I want to know the moment we make contact.
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
Yes, Prime Minister.
He nods once, sharply, and leaves the room without another word. The PM watches him go, then turns to the map.
PRIME MINISTER
There's one more thing. I want contingency plans for the worst case. If the Russians escalate — if they attack our ships, if they seize a British vessel in retaliation — I want options ready before this goes public. Not just military options. Economic, diplomatic, cyber. I want the full menu. And I want the Americans briefed in person, not on a phone call. I'll ask the Foreign Secretary to arrange a visit to Washington. Tomorrow.
DEPUTY PRIME MINISTER
The markets will react before the Foreign Secretary lands. We need to prepare a narrative that doesn't spook investors. "Calm resolve, not confrontation." That's the line.
PRIME MINISTER
That's the line. And make sure the Treasury is ready to stabilise the pound if it moves. I don't want a currency crisis on top of a maritime crisis.
DEFENCE SECRETARY
And what about the media? The Russian state media will be all over this. They'll say we're the aggressors. They'll say we're pirating their ships. We need to get ahead of that.
PRIME MINISTER
I'll make a statement at 07:00. Before the markets open. Before the Russians can spin their version. We'll release the satellite imagery. We'll show the fake manifests. We'll let the public see exactly what we're dealing with. Transparency is our best weapon.
DEPUTY PRIME MINISTER
And if the imagery is classified?
PRIME MINISTER
Then we declassify it. This is an extraordinary situation. The public has a right to know why their Navy is doing what it's doing.
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
Prime Minister, with respect — releasing operational imagery before the operation is complete could compromise the boarding. The Russians will see what we see. They'll know exactly where our assets are.
PRIME MINISTER
Then we release what we can without compromising the operation. I want the public on our side before the first shot is fired, real or imagined. That's not negotiable.
📍 23:14 — The Decision
PRIME MINISTER
Here's where I land. We don't board yet. But we stop shadowing and start escorting — in reverse. We place HMS Diamond and HMS Mersey between the Russian group and the Channel. We tell Moscow their corridor is closed. If they want to go north, around Scotland, that's their choice. They do not go through the Channel while they're carrying weapons components and threatening British citizens.
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
That's a blockade.
PRIME MINISTER
It's a redirect. A blockade is an act of war. This is a traffic management decision. We inform NATO. We inform the French. We give Moscow two hours to comply before we manoeuvre to block their line of advance.
DEFENCE SECRETARY
And if they don't comply?
PRIME MINISTER
Then we make them stop. Gwyn — can you hold that position?
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
I can hold it for as long as you want. But I need the legal cover, and I need to know what happens if a Russian captain decides to test the buoyancy of my ship with his hull.
PRIME MINISTER
Legal cover is being drafted now. The Attorney General is on standby. Rules of engagement: fire only if fired upon, but do not allow the task group to pass. That's the instruction.
DEPUTY PRIME MINISTER
And the message to the public?
PRIME MINISTER
We tell the truth. We say the Royal Navy has identified a Russian task group carrying sanctions-breached material. We say we have redirected them from the Channel pending investigation. We don't call it a blockade. We don't call it an act of war. We call it what it is — the Navy doing its job.
DEFENCE SECRETARY
And if Putin decides this is his Churchill moment?
PRIME MINISTER
Then we find out whether the Russians want to fight a conventional war in the North Sea or not. Because I'm not letting them sail past Dover with impunity, and I'm not letting them arm their war machine while we watch.
He drains his glass. The lamp is flickering. Outside, a siren passes in the distance — Chatham, perhaps, or Gillingham.
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
I'll prepare the order. Two hours from now, the Russian group will be informed that their passage through the Strait of Dover is not available. After that — it's their move.
PRIME MINISTER
Good. Let's get some sleep. Tomorrow is going to be long.
The room empties. The lamp stays on. The map on the desk shows the Channel, the North Sea, and a line of Russian ships heading west.
📍 23:31 — Worst Case
PRIME MINISTER
Let's say the worst happens. Let's say one of their ships doesn't just posture — let's say it actually fires on a British vessel. Or worse, hits the Diamond or the Mersey. What's the immediate response?
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
We don't wait for orders from London if a ship is under active attack. The commanding officer on scene has standing authority to defend his vessel and any British personnel. That's basic rules of engagement. If a Russian destroyer fires on HMS Diamond, Diamond returns fire. That's not a decision for this room — that's a decision for the captain, and it's already been made.
DEFENCE SECRETARY
But what happens after that first exchange? Because once a shot is fired, we're in a war. Not a cold war, not a maritime confrontation — a real, shooting war with a nuclear power. And I need to know what the end-state looks like.
PRIME MINISTER
That's what I'm asking. What's the escalation ladder? If Diamond returns fire and disables the Russian ship — not sinks it, disables it — what does Moscow do? Do they sink one of our frigates? Do they go after the Mersey? Do they open a second front in the Baltic?
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
The most likely immediate response is asymmetric. They don't fight us ship-to-ship in the Channel — they know they'd lose that exchange. They'd go after our undersea cables. They'd launch cyberattacks on the City. They'd seize a British-flagged vessel in the Mediterranean. They'd activate proxies in the Baltics. That's their doctrine — asymmetric, below the threshold of open war, but painful enough to make us reconsider.
DEPUTY PRIME MINISTER
And the markets?
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
The markets would crater. Not because of the shooting itself — the Channel is narrow, and a single exchange wouldn't close the Strait. But because uncertainty is the market's natural enemy. Investors would flee to dollars, gold, Swiss francs. The pound would drop. Insurance premiums for maritime cargo would triple overnight. The City would be dealing with the second- and third-order effects for months.
PRIME MINISTER
So the economic damage from a limited exchange could be worse than the military damage?
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
In many ways, yes. A damaged warship can be repaired. A shattered market takes longer to rebuild, and the social cost is higher. Unemployment, inflation, housing crisis — all of those get worse when the pound stumbles.
DEFENCE SECRETARY
But here's the thing — if we don't respond forcefully after a Russian attack, what message does that send? We'd be telling every adversary in the world that British ships are fair game. That the Royal Navy is a paper tiger. That we'll take the first punch and then ask for a negotiation. That's not deterrence. That's suicide.
PRIME MINISTER
I know. But I also know that a full-scale naval war with Russia is not winnable in the conventional sense. They have more ships, more submarines, more anti-ship missiles. We have quality, not quantity. And we have the alliance. The question is whether the alliance holds after the first British ship is hit.
DEPUTY PRIME MINISTER
That's the real question. If a Russian missile hits HMS Diamond, and we respond, do the Americans come in? Or do they stay on the sidelines and let us fight our own war? Because if they don't come in, we're in a very difficult position. We can't win a sustained naval conflict with Russia alone. We just can't.
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
Let me be precise about what NATO Article 5 means in this context. An attack on a British warship in international waters is an attack on a NATO member. That's clear. But the response doesn't have to be symmetrical. We could invoke Article 5 and then choose the level of our response. We could impose sanctions, cyberattacks, diplomatic expulsion. We don't have to declare war. But we do have to respond in a way that makes clear the attack was a mistake — or a crime — that won't be repeated.
DEFENCE SECRETARY
And what if Russia says it was an accident? That their ship malfunctioned, that their captain panicked, that it was a navigation error? They've done it before. They'll do it again. And half the world will believe them, because people want to believe that wars don't start by accident.
PRIME MINISTER
Then we need evidence. That's why the satellite imagery, the intercepts, the boarding documentation — all of it has to be ready. Not just for Parliament. For the world. We need to show, incontrovertibly, that this was not an accident. That the Russians planned this, that they escalated deliberately, that they left us no choice.
He opens the red folder and slides a photograph across the desk. It shows a Russian destroyer's gun turret trained on a British patrol vessel, close enough to read the name.
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
That was taken forty minutes ago. The Levchenko group is still holding position, but their weapons systems are active. They're not just watching anymore. They're preparing.
DEPUTY PRIME MINISTER
Then we need to make a decision about the rules of engagement. Are we telling our ships to hold fire until they're actually hit? Or are we telling them to fire if a Russian weapon system is activated?
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
The current ROE is: fire only if fired upon. That's the legal minimum. But I want to change it.
PRIME MINISTER
To what?
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
To: fire if a Russian weapon system is actively targeting a British vessel. That gives our commanders more latitude. It means they don't have to wait for the first impact. It means they can stop the attack before it starts.
DEFENCE SECRETARY
That's a big change. That moves us from reactive to pre-emptive. And if a Russian captain makes a mistake — if his radar locks on accidentally, if his weapons system glitches — we've just given our commanders permission to start a war over a technical fault.
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
I understand the risk. But the current ROE assumes the Russians are rational actors who won't fire first. I'm telling you, based on eighteen months of watching them, that assumption is no longer safe. They are testing boundaries. They are looking for a reaction. If we make it clear that any activation of their weapons systems will be met with immediate force, they may think twice.
PRIME MINISTER
It's a gamble. But it may be the only way to avoid a bigger exchange later. I'll authorize the change — but with a caveat. I want every Russian warning shot, every weapons activation, every aggressive manoeuvre logged and timestamped. I want a full audit trail. And I want the Attorney General's office to review the ROE language before it goes out.
DEPUTY PRIME MINISTER
And while we're tightening the military screws, what about the diplomatic track? Are we still pursuing a negotiated solution? Because if we change the ROE and the Russians respond by escalating further, we've closed the door on talks.
PRIME MINISTER
We keep the diplomatic channel open. But we don't let it slow us down. The Foreign Secretary should make one more call to the Russian ambassador tonight. A final warning: if they don't stand down by 02:00, we implement the redirect. After that, it's a military operation, not a negotiation.
DEFENCE SECRETARY
And what do we say to the families if it goes wrong? If a British sailor dies?
PRIME MINISTER
We tell them the truth. That their son or daughter was doing a dangerous job because the country asked it of them. That they died defending the rules that keep this country safe. And we make sure that the person responsible — whether it's a Russian captain or a Russian minister — is held accountable. That's the only language they understand.
The room goes quiet again. The PM closes the red folder and sets it aside.
PRIME MINISTER
One more thing. I want a direct line to the White House. If the first shot is fired, I want the President on the phone within five minutes. Not his aides — him. I need to know that the alliance is real, that it's not just press releases. If Washington hesitates, we need to know that before we commit our ships.
CHIEF OF THE DEFENCE STAFF
I'll make sure the military liaison is ready. But the political call has to come from you, Prime Minister. The Americans respond to strength and clarity. If you sound uncertain, they'll hesitate. If you sound like you know exactly what you're doing, they'll follow.
DEPUTY PRIME MINISTER
Then let's be clear. We are not looking for a war. But we are not afraid of one either. The message to Moscow is simple: you sail past Dover, you carry weapons components, you threaten British citizens — and we will stop you. If you fire, we will respond. If you escalate, we will out-escalate you in the only place that matters: the alliance. You are not the only power with partners.
PRIME MINISTER
Good. That's the message. Now let's get some rest. Tomorrow, we either redirect those ships or we find out whether this is really a crisis or just another test. Either way, we'll be ready.

One last thing. If this goes bad — if we lose a ship, if we lose men and women — I want the full honours. I want the repatriation ceremony at the Memorial Gate. I want the Union Flag on the coffin. They don't get to kill British service members and then sit in our capital and pretend it was an accident.

Agreed. Expulsion within hours. And a full military honours funeral. The public needs to see that we value the people we send into danger. That's not politics — that's respect.

DEPUTY PRIME MINISTER
The Treasury is already moving. I've asked the Bank of England to prepare a liquidity backstop for the City. If the Russians attack, we don't want a financial panic on top of a military one. The markets will react, but we can blunt the worst of it.

Good. And the cyber cell?

Active. We've already seen probing attacks on the NHS spine and the power grid. If the Russians go kinetic, the cyber cell goes active. We'll take down their banking systems, their air traffic control, their satellite links. Not permanently — just enough to make the cost real.
PRIME MINISTER

DEPUTY PRIME MINISTER
Already done. The legal team has been working on it for hours. Every target is vetted. Every strike is proportionate. We're not looking for a cyberspace apocalypse — just a clear message that we can reach them too.
