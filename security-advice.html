Security Advice — Don't Store Bank Details

A simple, practical guide to keeping your accounts safe from token theft, browser saving, and account takeover.

← Back to Medway Ghosts
Quick message: If your browser offers to save your bank details, say no. The convenience is not worth the risk.

What Happened

You stored bank details in a browser token. Someone got into your Odin account and tried to pay for a cinema ticket. Barclays caught it, but the breach started because sensitive data was saved locally in a place that wasn't as secure as it looked.

This is a common attack path. Tokens and saved form data are easy targets for malware, browser extensions, phishing, or anyone with physical access to your device.

The Core Rule

Never store bank details in your browser. That means:

  • Don't save card numbers, expiry dates, or security codes in Chrome, Firefox, Edge, Safari, or any browser password manager.
  • Don't paste bank details into tokens, notes apps, or password fields that aren't your bank's actual site.
  • Don't reuse passwords across banking, email, and shopping sites.

A compromised password is bad enough on its own. If the browser also has your card saved, the attacker doesn't need to steal anything extra — they just use what's already there. That's likely what happened here.

Why Browsers Are Risky

  • Browser sync leaks: If your Google or Microsoft account is compromised, synced passwords and autofill data go with it.
  • Malware can read autofill: Some trojans specifically target saved card data in browsers.
  • Extensions are untrusted: A single malicious extension can read every form you fill.
  • Tokens are persistent: A stolen session token can let an attacker act as you without needing your password again.

What To Do Right Now

  • Go into your browser settings and delete all saved payment methods.
  • Revoke any suspicious sessions on your Odin account and any linked accounts.
  • Change your Odin password and enable two-factor authentication if it's available.
  • Contact your bank and ask them to flag your account for any unusual activity.
  • Check your email for password-reset attempts or unknown forwards.

Better Alternatives

  • Use your bank's official app for payments instead of browser checkout where possible.
  • Use a dedicated password manager with a strong master password and 2FA — not the one built into your browser.
  • Use virtual card numbers if your bank offers them. These can be cancelled without affecting your main card.
  • Check URLs before entering details — make sure it's your bank's real site, not a phishing clone.

Bottom Line

Convenience is the attacker's entry point. If a browser prompts you to save bank details, treat it as a security warning, not a helpful feature. The few seconds of typing are worth the protection.
